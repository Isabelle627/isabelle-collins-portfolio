01
The proposal

A room built from uncertain memories.

The Room That Remembers is a short browser puzzle game in which the player explores a familiar-looking room that gradually becomes impossible to trust.

The player experience

Look closer. Question what changed.

The player arrives in a quiet bedroom containing a small collection of ordinary objects: a mirror, a photograph, a clock, a chair, and a closed door. At first, the room feels recognizable. As the player inspects objects, details begin to contradict one another. A photograph changes, the clock moves in the wrong direction, and the reflection does not always match the room.

The player is not asked to defeat an enemy. Instead, they investigate the room, compare clues, and decide which version of the memory they believe.

Design at a glance

Genre: Surreal point-and-click puzzle

Platform: Desktop web browser

Primary input: Mouse click / tap to inspect objects

Audience: Players who enjoy short atmospheric stories and environmental puzzles

Estimated play time: 3–5 minutes

MemoryPerceptionUncertaintySurrealism
Core design goal: Make the player question the reliability of what they see through one simple interaction—clicking and examining objects—rather than relying on complicated controls or a long explanation.
02
Research / case study

Learning from another browser game.

We Become What We Behold by Nicky Case is the case study for this proposal. Its simple camera interaction makes the player an active part of how a story is framed and understood.

Game studied

We Become What We Behold

Creator: Nicky Case

Year: 2016

Format: Short browser game

Interaction: Move the camera and click to photograph moments.

Play the game / creator page ↗

Why this reference?

I selected this game because it uses a very small set of actions to communicate a larger idea about media, attention, and social behavior. The player does not directly control the characters. Instead, the player chooses what to frame and share. That creates an interesting relationship between player agency and the consequences shown in the game.

Design principle I want to study: A player's attention can become part of the narrative. In my game, the objects the player chooses to inspect will reveal different pieces of a fragmented memory.

04
Narrative / concept

What if a place remembered differently?

For this project, hallucination means an experience in which perception becomes unreliable: what appears to be true does not always match the evidence around it.

Story premise

A familiar room with unfamiliar memories

The player wakes in a quiet room and notices a photograph on the desk. It seems to show a memory of this place, but small details do not match what is currently visible. The player explores the room to understand why the objects seem to remember different versions of the same event.

As the player inspects more objects, the room becomes less stable. The mirror reflects an altered space, the clock suggests a different time, and the photograph changes. The player gradually realizes that the room is not a reliable record of the past.

Player role

The investigator

The player is an observer trying to assemble a coherent memory from incomplete clues. They are not given a definitive narrator. Their task is to notice contradictions and decide what to believe.

Intended emotional arc
ArriveFamiliar and quiet
WonderNotice a mismatch
DoubtFind contradictions
DiscoverQuestion the memory
LeaveAccept uncertainty

The experience should feel curious first, then subtly unsettling. I want players to leave thinking about how memory and perception can shape the stories they tell themselves.

05
Interaction / player journey

One action, three stages, a shifting reality.

The main interaction is click-to-inspect. Each object responds with a visual change, a short text clue, or a contradiction that moves the player forward.

Player journey flowchart
OpeningEnter the room and read the first instruction
Stage 1Inspect ordinary objects
Stage 2Compare conflicting clues
Stage 3Reveal the room's contradiction
EndingChoose what memory to carry forward

The player can inspect objects in any order within a stage. Finding the required clues unlocks the next stage.

Storyboard / key screens

01 / OPENING

The quiet room

Goal: Understand the space. Input: Click “begin.” Feedback: A short instruction appears.

02 / STAGE ONE

Objects feel normal

Goal: Explore. Input: Click the mirror, clock, or photograph. Feedback: Each object reveals a clue.

03 / STAGE TWO

Details disagree

Goal: Compare clues. Input: Inspect objects again. Feedback: The room changes and descriptions conflict.

04 / STAGE THREE + END

The memory shifts

Goal: Interpret the clues. Input: Select one of two memory fragments. Feedback: The ending reflects the player's choice but preserves uncertainty.

Scope control: The prototype will use one room, a small number of clickable objects, short text, and simple state changes. This keeps the game achievable within the two-week build period.
06
Art direction / sound guide

Soft, dreamlike, and slightly wrong.

The visual language begins calm and familiar, then introduces subtle distortions. The interface should remain simple so players focus on the room and its clues.

Color direction

Muted colors with selective contrast

Deep plum
Violet
Lavender
Dusty rose
Warm gold

Deep plum anchors the room. Lavender and dusty rose create a soft dreamlike atmosphere. Warm gold highlights clues that deserve attention. Color shifts will signal that something has changed.

Typography + interface

Quiet, readable, and minimal

Typography: A restrained serif for titles and a clean sans-serif for instructions and object descriptions.

Interface: Small labels, clear hover states, and a simple clue panel. Clickable objects should be visually discoverable without adding distracting buttons everywhere.

Motion: Slow fades, slight object movement, and brief image distortion. Motion should communicate a change in perception, not decorate every action.

Visual reference 01

We Become What We Behold — Nicky Case

Reference: Game page.

What I am studying: Clear, minimal visual communication and how a simple framing mechanic supports the concept. I will not copy its characters or interface.

Visual reference 02

Monument Valley — ustwo games

Reference: Official project website.

What I am studying: Impossible architecture, controlled composition, and visual perspective. I will translate the principle into small changes in the room rather than recreate its art.

Sound direction

Sound should make the player notice absence as well as presence.

Ambient layer: A very quiet room tone, such as a soft low hum. Interaction feedback: A gentle click or muted chime when an object is inspected. Distortion cue: A brief reversed or softened tone when an object contradicts an earlier clue. Silence: Use a short pause before a major reveal to make the change feel intentional.

Audio will be optional, with a visible sound toggle. The game must remain understandable and playable with sound turned off. I plan to use original or properly licensed audio and credit every asset used.

Reference credits: Nicky Case, We Become What We Behold; ustwo games, Monument Valley. These are research references, not assets copied into the project. Any image, sound, or other third-party asset used in the final game will be separately credited and checked for permission.

07
Production plan

From proposal to playable experience.

The build is intentionally small: one environment, one main interaction, three short stages, and a clear ending.

Week 2 / Build the prototype

Create the playable core

  • Set up the HTML, CSS, and JavaScript structure.
  • Build the room layout and clickable objects.
  • Implement click-to-inspect interactions and clue text.
  • Create the opening and three short stages.
  • Add basic transitions and an ending state.
  • Make the layout usable on desktop and mobile-sized screens.

Deliverable: A playable prototype that demonstrates the complete beginning-to-ending journey.

Week 3 / Test, refine, publish

Improve clarity and atmosphere

  • Ask several people to play without explaining the story first.
  • Observe whether they understand what can be clicked and what to do next.
  • Record confusion, missed clues, and pacing issues.
  • Adjust instructions, contrast, timing, and object feedback.
  • Test sound controls and confirm the game works without audio.
  • Fix bugs and publish the final game on the portfolio website.

Deliverable: A tested, refined, published browser game with a working URL.

How success will be evaluated

What I want players to experience

Players should understand the main interaction quickly, discover at least one meaningful contradiction in each stage, and reach an ending that makes them reconsider what they saw. The game should be short, accessible, visually consistent, and understandable without sound.

Closing statement

Can you trust what the room remembers?

The Room That Remembers turns the act of looking into the central mechanic. By exploring a space that changes its story, players experience how uncertain perception can become—and how meaning is built from the details we choose to believe.
